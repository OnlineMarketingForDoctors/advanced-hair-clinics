Hair Loss: Causes, Types, Treatment and Therapy

Hair loss can affect only your scalp or your entire body, with temporary or permanent results. Learn how it develops, what causes it, its pathological forms, methods of diagnosis, treatment and therapy, and what to do.

Hair loss occurs when new hair doesn’t grow to replace fallen hair. An average person typically loses 50 to 100 hairs per day. However, this isn’t noticeable because new hair is growing simultaneously.

Anyone can lose hair on their head, but it’s more common in men than in women. Baldness usually refers to excessive hair loss from your scalp. Hereditary hair loss is the most common cause of baldness.

Some choose to let their hair loss progress without treatment or concealment. Others try to cover it with hairstyles, hats, or wigs. Most choose one of the available treatments to prevent further hair loss or restore hair growth.

How hair grows

The Hair Life Cycle

Hair loss is a natural phenomenon. Each hair follicle has its own life cycle that can be affected by age, diseases, and a wide variety of other factors. The hair life cycle lasts from 2 to 7 years and is divided into the following phases:

  1. 80–90%

    Anagen growth phase

    This is the active phase of hair growth. Up to 80-90% of scalp hairs are growing at any time.

  2. ≈3%

    Catagen transition phase

    During several weeks, hair growth slows as follicles shrink and hair stops growing. About 3% of all hairs are in this phase at any time.

  3. 6–8%

    Telogen resting phase

    The hair follicle is in complete rest and no longer grows. About 6%-8% of all hairs are resting at any time, and this phase usually lasts 2-4 months.

Why it happens

Causes of Hair Loss

Various causes, such as diseases, poor nutrition, hormonal disorders, or excessive stress, can cause hair loss. It can be the result of heredity or simply be a normal evolution of the aging process.

If you’re experiencing problems with thinning hair or baldness, your dermatologist must identify the root of the problem to determine appropriate treatment.

01

Heredity

Hair loss due to heredity is the most common cause of hair loss. It’s not really a disease but a natural condition caused by a combination of genetics, hormone levels, and the aging process.

Contrary to popular belief that it’s the cap you wear, your shampoo, or the new hair dye you bought, your parents’ genes play a very important role and may be responsible for your thinning hair. These genes combined with your hormones and environmental factors set the foundation for potential hair loss and baldness development.

However, the exact mode of inheritance and the relative importance of each parent remains unclear. Both men and women experience this type of hair loss, which is the most common cause of hair loss worldwide.

  • In men, it’s called male pattern hair loss.
  • Women experience female pattern hair loss.

Regardless of whether it develops in men or women, the medical term is androgenetic alopecia, which is also known as MPB (male-pattern baldness).

Regardless of the term you use, the genes you inherited cause shrinkage of your hair follicles where hair grows, and eventually hair growth stops. The shrinkage can begin as early as your teens, but usually starts at a much older age.

In menWhen a man has hereditary hair loss, the first sign is often a receding hairline in the front of the head or a bald spot on top.

In womenIn women, the first sign of hereditary hair loss is usually overall thinning or widening of the part.

02

Medical Conditions

Certain diseases can also cause hair loss, such as eating disorders, iron deficiency anemia, diabetes, autoimmune diseases like lupus and alopecia areata, as well as skin conditions like psoriasis and dermatitis.

Other cases are due to scalp conditions such as fungal infections, folliculitis, and other inflammations.

If you’re experiencing hair loss, talk to your doctor to rule out such diseases.

03

Medications

Hair loss can be due to side effects of certain medications used for acne, cancer, arthritis, gout, heart problems, high blood pressure, and depression. Also, if you exercise, taking anabolic steroids can cause you to lose more hair.

The severity of hair loss depends on the type of medication and dosage, as well as your sensitivity to that medication.

However, in most cases, hair loss is reversible once you stop taking the medication.

Hair loss also occurs after chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and drug treatments, as well as in chronic conditions.

If you’re currently taking any of these medications and notice hair loss, contact your doctor. Remember, never stop taking a medication before discussing it with your doctor.

04

Hormonal Changes

Hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, meaning the production of more or less hormones than normal, can cause hormonal imbalance. Hormones from the thyroid gland help regulate almost all body functions, including hair growth.

Hyperparathyroidism is the pathological condition caused by overactivity of one or more of the 4 parathyroid glands.

Controlling any of these thyroid conditions with proper treatment can regulate hormones, prevent hair loss, and restore hair growth.

Pregnancy

Especially in women, major hormonal changes such as pregnancy, lactation, menstrual disorders, polycystic ovary syndrome, and menopause can cause intense hair loss, which is reversible in most cases.

Hormonal imbalances that occur after pregnancy and childbirth can cause hair loss. It takes some time after pregnancy for hormone levels to return to normal, so it’s not unusual for mothers to notice thin hair or even baldness after giving birth. This usually happens about 3 months after the baby’s birth. Don’t worry, as the rest of your body recovers, your hair follicles will recover too. The hair loss is only temporary, and your hair will grow back.

Testosterone and Dihydrotestosterone

Without testosterone and dihydrotestosterone (DHT), you wouldn’t have any of your male characteristics, but unfortunately, these hormones play a major role in androgenetic alopecia.

In the body, testosterone is converted to its more “active” form, dihydrotestosterone, with the help of an enzyme called 5-alpha reductase. Dihydrotestosterone acts on various organs of the body, including hair follicles. The 5-α reductase enzyme is responsible for converting testosterone to dihydrotestosterone 3 times more in the frontal area than in the occipital region.

It’s not the production of dihydrotestosterone itself that causes hair loss, but the hereditary sensitivity to it. Men with genetically high sensitivity to dihydrotestosterone will experience earlier weakening of their hair follicles.

Essentially, dihydrotestosterone shrinks hair follicles and shortens the hair growth cycle. This makes hair grow and appear thinner and more fragile, as well as fall out faster. It can also slow down the growth of new hairs in the follicles when old hairs fall out.

Certain habits can increase dihydrotestosterone levels. Sometimes the desire to get stronger at the gym works against people who are sensitive to androgenetic alopecia. Engaging in behaviors that increase dihydrotestosterone production, such as taking creatine supplements, weight training, or taking anabolic steroids, have the potential to increase hair loss.

Another common cause of hormonal imbalance causing hair loss is polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), which creates cysts in a woman’s ovaries. These cysts, although not dangerous, limit estrogen production, resulting in increased testosterone levels in the female body, increasing androgen levels.

Discontinuing certain types of contraceptive medications can cause temporary hormonal imbalance. Women who develop a hormonal imbalance can experience hair thinning (or hair loss) on their scalp.

05

Stress

Scientific studies have shown that prolonged stress can contribute to some hair loss conditions.

When the body undergoes severe physical stress, such as in serious accidents, surgery, burns, or severe illness, it can cause shock to the hair follicles. The natural hair growth cycle can be disrupted, resulting in hair loss, often initially in the form of thinning hair. Such stress can result in up to 75% of hair falling out, sometimes months after the event.

Emotional stress can also lead to hair loss. When we face a life-changing event, such as psychological stress from divorce or separation or poor living conditions with our partner, unemployment or other financial problems, loss of home, serious illness or death of a loved one, or depression, severe emotional stress can disrupt the normal hair growth cycle.

Major emotional stressors can cause temporary hair loss, but once stress is controlled, normal hair growth usually resumes.

There are many long-term observations and statistical studies proving that stress can lead to hair loss. However, scientists haven’t yet fully decoded the biological mechanism by which chronic stress damages hair follicle stem cells. According to recent Harvard University research, stress causes disturbances in stress-related hormones, especially increased levels of the stress hormone cortisol. Cortisol prolongs the resting phase of hair follicles or even returns hair follicles to this phase. As a result, hairs loosen from their position and fall out.

06

Trauma or Burns

Localized hair loss, usually permanent in most cases, can occur in cases of traumatic alopecia, as a consequence of trauma or burns to the scalp, face, or body.

Traction alopecia and trichotillomania are also classified in the category of hair loss from traumatic alopecia.

Traction alopecia occurs in cases where there is excessive pulling of hair in various hairstyles and burdensome hairdressing processes, such as ponytails, tight buns, braids, extensions, etc.

Trichotillomania is a condition where some people pull or pluck their hair without realizing it. The main cause of trichotillomania is anxiety and stress. It usually begins in childhood or adolescence and can last for several months or many years. Most pull hair from the head, but some also pull hair from their beard, eyebrows, or eyelashes.

07

Nutrition and Diet

Poor nutrition or an exhaustive diet intensify the problem of hair loss. Lack of proteins, vitamins, fatty acids, and trace elements/minerals such as zinc or iron, especially if continued over an extended period, can lead to diffuse alopecia. Fortunately, this condition is generally reversible, and adopting healthier, less restrictive eating habits will allow your hair to grow back. If your hair is falling out, see here which vitamins help against hair loss.

08

Environmental Conditions

Modern environmental living conditions can accelerate hair loss. Exposure to an environment burdened with dust and pollution, excessive sun exposure, and frequent contact with sea or chlorinated water, or even with acidic rainwater, can lead to hair thinning.

09

Aging

Whether you have androgenetic alopecia or not, thinning and hair loss is a normal part of aging for everyone. This doesn’t mean you’ll lose all your hair as a result of aging. However, as hormones in the body change with age, the natural rate of hair growth slows down, and the number of hairs on your head will decrease.

Women experience this too, and it’s one reason you’ll notice many elderly women with thin hair, especially after menopause.

Types

Pathological Forms of Hair Loss

  • Androgenetic alopecia

    Is the most common type of hair loss and is caused by hormonal disorders and genetic predisposition.

  • Alopecia areata

    An autoimmune disorder where the immune system attacks hair follicles, rendering them inactive.

  • Diffuse Alopecia (Telogen effluvium)

    Sudden, usually temporary hair loss that can be caused by physical stress or psychological causes.

  • Cicatricial alopecia

    Due to endogenous causes, with follicular lichen being its most common form. However, it’s also due to external factors such as burns, injuries, or inflammation.

Treatment

Treatment and Therapy for Hair Loss

Hair loss treatment can be divided into two major categories: conservative treatment which includes medication, and surgical.

Conservative&Surgical
Conservative

Medications for Hair Loss Treatment

Medications, as well as some products, can also help slow down or prevent hair loss.

  • Minoxidil, which prolongs the anagen phase of hair, has proven to be one of the most effective pharmaceutical substances against hair loss.
  • Topical application of corticosteroids to the scalp aimed at combating inflammation that causes hair follicle degeneration. Non-specific treatment is mainly applied in cases of hair loss of unclear etiology, where there is no causal treatment, such as in cases of diffuse, scarring, or alopecia areata.
  • Anti-hair loss products, such as shampoos, lotions, conditioners, serums with active ingredients and natural herb extracts that are dermatologically tested.
Conservative

Other Conservative Treatments

  • Autologous hair mesotherapy, also known as “autologous growth factors” hair loss treatment, is a new, promising treatment based on harnessing the patient’s own healing and growth forces.
  • LLLT laser therapy aims to improve cases of androgenetic and diffuse alopecia by improving blood circulation and hair nutrition. However, it’s more effective in combination with other treatments, and its effectiveness varies depending on individual hair loss characteristics. It’s not the ideal solution for hair loss due to increased testosterone levels.

Surgical Treatment

Dietary Supplements for Hair Loss

Supporting conservative treatment against hair loss, numerous products are used, mainly shampoos, lotions, or ampoules with nutrients, dietary supplements with vitamins, trace elements, amino acids, etc.

Generally, the effectiveness of these means in treating hair loss is controversial, but it’s considered that proper and balanced nutrition, rich in plant fibers and vitamins, especially B group vitamins, trace elements such as zinc, and natural products like aloe, contribute to proper hair nutrition and health.

Diagnosis

Hair Loss Diagnosis

Diagnosis should be made by a dermatologist specialized in hair loss. Hair loss has many causes, but proper diagnosis will reveal what’s causing your hair loss and determine if your hair:

  • Falls gradually or suddenly
  • Is weak
  • Can regenerate on its own
  • Requires treatment to grow back
  • Needs immediate care to prevent permanent hair loss

Before proceeding with diagnosis, the dermatologist will perform a physical examination and ask about your diet, medical and family history, and hair care routine. Then, they may subject you to tests, such as:

  1. Blood test

    Helps reveal medical conditions or vitamin or other component deficiencies that may contribute to hair loss.

  2. Pull test

    The dermatologist gently pulls several dozen hairs to see how many come out. This helps determine the stage of hair loss.

  3. Biopsy

    Taking samples from the skin or hairs removed from the scalp to determine if an infection is causing hair loss.

  4. Microscopic examination (Trichogram)

    Examination with a special microscope to identify possible hair shaft disorders.

  5. Phototrichogram

    The most modern diagnostic method using a micro-camera and special computer software to monitor response to therapeutic treatment and accurate measurement of hair density and diameter.

  6. Genetic testing

    The examination is done to detect the presence of genes in the patient’s DNA that are associated with the likelihood of developing androgenetic alopecia.

Prevention

Prevention of Hair Loss

Proper hair care will help combat hair loss. Avoid pulling when brushing and styling, especially when your hair is wet. A wide-toothed comb can help avoid pulling hair. Avoid intensive styling and overuse of tools like flat irons or curling irons, and reduce as much as possible the use of accessories like hair ties or clips.

Ask your doctor about medications and supplements you’re taking that might cause hair loss. Also ask them about available treatments, such as minoxidil or autologous hair mesotherapy. Protect your hair from sunlight and other sources of ultraviolet light. Stop smoking. Some studies show a correlation between smoking and baldness in men.

Also, for people with sensitive, fine, and weak hair with a hereditary history of hair loss, the use of mild shampoos with neutral pH, suitable for daily washing, is generally recommended.

Questions & answers

FAQs

Answers about hair loss from the dermatologists of Advanced Hair Clinics.

Ask your own question

How is hair loss diagnosed?

The diagnosis of hair loss should always be performed by a qualified dermatologist. Key elements in the diagnosis of androgenetic alopecia are the post-puberty history of occurrence, the gradual thinning in specific areas, and the family history regarding hair loss and alopecia. At Advanced Hair Clinics, hair loss is diagnosed or confirmed with a trichogram, using a special microcamera and software.

The special diagnostic photographic imaging and digital analysis tool “Follysis” that is used in our clinic, is one of the most comprehensive and accurate tools for the diagnosis of hair loss and the monitoring of response to treatment, allowing accurate measurements of hair density and hair strand diameter in each area.

Certain cases of hair loss, such as alopecia areata or cicatricial alopecia, may require special blood tests (hormonological tests, iron, vitamins, minerals, etc.) or a skin biopsy (punch biopsy) for the purpose of differential diagnosis.

Why does hair loss occur?

The life cycle of hair comprises of: the anagen phase (in which hair growth takes place), the catagen phase (in which the hair stops growing and becomes detached from its root), the telogen phase (shedding). Hair loss occurs when the duration of the anagen phase becomes shorter and the hairs quickly pass into the telogen phase (telogen hair loss or telogen effluvium). In this case, the hair loss is diffuse, i.e. not located in a particular area, but spread over the entire scalp, causing reduction of the hair density and thinning of the hair strands. This form of hair loss can occur after giving birth, due to chronic infections or after administration of certain medications.

Other cases of hair loss are due to diseases of the scalp, such as fungal infections, folliculitis and other inflammations. Hair thinning can also be caused by malfunction of the thyroid gland (hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism) and in hyperparathyroidism and other chronic diseases. Hair loss also occurs after chemotherapy, radiation therapy and certain medications, and in a number of chronic diseases. In these situations, hair loss subsides after identifying and eliminating the cause.

Especially in women, major hormonal changes such as pregnancy, lactation, menstrual disorders, polycystic ovarian syndrome, menopause, etc., can lead to severe hair loss which is reversible in most cases. Localised hair loss of a usually permanent nature may occur in cases of traumatic alopecia, caused by injuries or burns to the scalp, face or body.

How can I tell if my hair loss is pathological?

Complete loss of hair at specific locations can be seen in alopecia areata (spot baldness) and in scars (cicatricial alopecia), such as scalp injuries or burns. Hair loss and creation of cicatricial patches is also seen in follicular lichen planus and lupus erythematosus. Characteristic feature of these pathological forms of hair loss is that they can affect any area of the scalp, even the constant hair growth zone, and evolve in an erratic and unforeseeable manner.

These conditions may be localised, small-scaled and relatively stable, but in rare cases can take a global form; in some cases of alopecia areata, complete hair loss may occur not only on the area of the scalp, but also on face, on eyelashes or even on the entire body. The cases of pathological hair loss are much rarer than androgenetic alopecia and constitute less than 10% of all cases of alopecia.

Does modern lifestyle affect hair health?

Important causes of hair loss in today’s era are the quite stressful way of life as well as intense emotions, stress, exposure to a dusty and polluted environment, excessive exposure to solar UV radiation, frequent contact with sea or chlorinated water.

Malnutrition can aggravate hair loss, as a lack of protein, vitamins and minerals such as iron or zinc intensify the problem. A proper diet that includes protein and iron can reverse hair loss.

Can intense hair styling lead to hair loss?

Traction hair loss caused by the excessive pulling of the hair due to various hairdressing and styling processes (e.g. ponytails, extensions, etc) can take place.

What is LLLT treatment, and what effectiveness does it have?

LLLT is a treatment for hair loss that uses a low-intensity laser. Although it is not used as often as other treatments, such as autologous hair mesotherapy, it is designed primarily for people who prefer to avoid injectable therapies. LLLT may have some positive effects in cases of hormonal changes, such as those seen during menopause or postpartum. However, it does not demonstrate the same effectiveness as autologous hair mesotherapy. Moreover, this treatment is unsuitable for people with advanced hair loss, as it cannot stimulate hair follicle regeneration.
